Trap placement from a site map

Map in. Traps out. Proof attached.

BioPath takes the layout of a site and the number of traps you can afford, and returns where to put them, with evidence anyone can check: how far the remotest corner is from a trap, how often a simulated wanderer is caught, and how a rule-of-thumb layout does on the same ground.

A research prototype built in 2026 for the University of Bristol's Early Career Enterprise Fellowship. Everything below runs on a synthetic map; the numbers are demo metrics, not field results.

Try it

Map

A synthetic layout inspired by a Cambridgeshire farm, 28 × 13 m in 1 m cells: yards divided by walls, with a few gaps. Colour is the walk to the nearest trap.

Rule of thumb is the package's own baseline: traps along walls first, at least 5 m apart. Caught counts 160 simulated random walks from random open cells, each pausing on about a quarter of its steps; the range is a 95% interval.

The page solves in your browser with a line-for-line port of the package's placement step: shortest walking distance, greedy choice, then swaps while any swap helps. A test in the repository checks that it places the same traps as the Python code. The package can also optimise the capture rate directly and test it against biased movement; that runs in Python.

Why traps, and why a map

It started close to home.

“Movement modelling is my route in; my grandfather’s strawberries are why it feels personal.” (From the June 2026 talk.)

A trap is where movement becomes evidence.

Lures attract, sticky cards catch, and monitoring points count what is happening in the crop row. BioPath asks where those points should go.

A few metres change the work that follows.

Better placement can mean fewer wasted checks, less chemical pressure and fewer weak explanations.

Movement is uncertain. The space shapes it.

Encounters are not random once space has structure: walls, corridors and gaps decide where a wanderer is likely to arrive first. That first arrival is the subject of the research behind BioPath, first-passage processes, and it is what the solver uses.

Coordinates, not just a picture.

A placement with numbers attached can be questioned: why these spots, and how good are they? The decision moves from gut feel alone to something a site can test.

The hard question is trust.

The fellowship moved the question from "can it work?" to "who would use it, pay for it and come back to it?", and from leading with the method to leading with the decision.

What exists

Solver
A Python package that reads a grid map with optional risk weights and places a given number of traps.
Proof bundle
Each benchmark run saves its traps, distances, capture estimates with intervals, scores under biased movement, and the rule-of-thumb and random baselines on the same map.
Interfaces
A command-line tool, a small web API, and this page.
Tests
Unit tests for the map reader, objectives, optimiser and capture model, and the browser-to-Python check.

The same answer, two thousand times

A scheduled job, set to run every two hours, reruns the benchmark on the demo farm: six traps, chosen for the worst of three movement scenarios, 160 simulated walks per scenario, fixed seed. Between 24 February and 15 November 2026 it was rerun 2,146 times on that map and returned the same answer every time: 48% caught (95% interval 40–56%), against 15% for the rule-of-thumb layout and 30% for random layouts on average.

What it is not

Not a product, and not field-tested. The maps are synthetic; the movement model and any risk weights are assumptions; the repository holds no field data. The demo metrics say what a pilot on a real site should measure, not what it would find.
